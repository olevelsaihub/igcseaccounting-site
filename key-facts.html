Key facts by topic

1 The accounting environment

Keeping computer accounting data safe
  • Data can be at risk in two main ways: it can be lost or damaged, or it can be seen or changed by people who should not have access.
  • Passwords (strong, changed regularly) stop unauthorised people from logging in to the accounting system.
  • A firewall blocks unauthorised access to the computer from outside, such as from the internet.
  • Anti-virus and anti-spyware software detect and remove harmful programs that could damage or steal data.
  • Regular backups, kept in a separate place (off-site or in the cloud), mean data can be restored if it is lost.
  • Other measures include encryption, giving staff access only to the parts they need, and keeping computers in locked rooms.

Open the lesson and practice

Public sector, private sector and sole traders
  • The public sector is the part of the economy owned and run by the government, such as state schools, hospitals and the police.
  • Public sector bodies are paid for by the government, mainly from taxes, and their main aim is to give a service to the community, not to earn a profit.
  • The private sector is made up of businesses owned by private individuals, such as sole traders and partnerships, and their main aim is to make a profit.
  • A sole trader is one person who owns the business, puts in the capital, takes all the decisions and keeps all of the profit.
  • Advantages of being a sole trader include: it is easy and cheap to set up, the owner has full control, decisions can be made quickly and the owner does not share the profit.
  • A 'difference' question needs two sides: say what one type of organisation does, then use 'whereas' to say what the other does.

Open the lesson and practice

The principles of professional ethics
  • Integrity: an accountant must be straightforward, honest and truthful in all work.
  • Objectivity: an accountant must not let bias, conflicts of interest or pressure from others affect their judgement; they make their own fair judgements.
  • Professional competence (and due care): an accountant must keep their knowledge and skills at the right level and work carefully.
  • Confidentiality: an accountant must not share information about a client or employer without permission, unless there is a legal duty to do so.
  • Professional behaviour: an accountant must obey laws and regulations and avoid actions that bring the profession into disrepute.
  • Accounting concepts (such as materiality or prudence) and made-up terms (such as duality) are not principles of professional ethics.

Open the lesson and practice

Naming and describing the accounting concepts
  • Accruals (matching): income and expenses are recorded in the period they belong to, not when cash is received or paid.
  • Business entity: the business is treated as separate from its owner, so the owner's private items are kept out of the business books.
  • Consistency: once a method is chosen (for example a depreciation method) it is used in the same way every year so results can be compared.
  • Materiality: items of very small value need not be recorded separately or treated as assets; they can simply be expensed.
  • Money measurement: only things that can be given a money value are recorded in the accounts.
  • Prudence: profits and assets should not be overstated and liabilities should not be understated; take the cautious figure.
  • Going concern (sometimes offered in multiple choice): the business is expected to keep trading for the foreseeable future.
  • Words such as confidentiality, integrity, objectivity, comparability, understandability and duality are not accounting concepts in this course.

Open the lesson and practice

Stakeholders and why they read the accounts
  • A stakeholder is any person or group with an interest in how a business is doing.
  • Internal stakeholders are inside the business: the owner, managers and employees.
  • External stakeholders are outside the business: customers, suppliers (trade payables), banks and other lenders, investors, the government, competitors and the local community.
  • Each stakeholder reads the financial statements for its own reason, linked to a decision it must make.
  • Typical interests: owner - is the business profitable; supplier - will we be paid; bank - can the loan and interest be repaid; employee - is my job safe; customer - will supplies continue; government - how much tax is due.
  • A good 'interest' names something specific the stakeholder wants to know, not a vague phrase such as 'to see how the business is doing'.

Open the lesson and practice

Technology in accounting: benefits, drawbacks and decisions
  • Accounting software records transactions once and automatically completes the double entry, updates ledgers and produces reports.
  • Spreadsheets use formulae to do repeated calculations automatically and can be updated quickly.
  • Main benefits: speed of processing, improved accuracy of calculations, up-to-date information, easy production of reports and financial statements, and saving staff time.
  • Main drawbacks: cost of hardware and software, cost and time of training, risk of data loss, viruses or hacking, and errors if wrong figures are typed in.
  • Employing a bookkeeper is an alternative: it adds a running wages expense that reduces profit each year, but brings skill without the owner needing training.
  • An 'Evaluate' or 'Advise' answer needs points on both sides, each developed with a consequence, and a final decision with a reason.

Open the lesson and practice

Spotting the concept behind an accounting treatment
  • Business entity: owner's private items (a private lunch, a personal computer, goods or stationery taken for personal use) are treated as drawings, not business expenses.
  • Materiality: cheap items such as a calculator, a brush or stationery are treated as expenses, not non-current assets, even if they last more than a year.
  • Consistency: the same depreciation method (or other method) is used every year.
  • Prudence: inventory is valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value; provisions for irrecoverable debts are made so that assets and profit are not overstated.
  • Accruals (matching): other payables and other receivables (accruals and prepayments) are adjusted so each year carries only its own income and expenses.
  • Money measurement: things with no reliable money value, such as staff skills and knowledge, are not shown as assets.
  • Depreciation is linked to accruals, prudence and consistency; irrecoverable debts and debt provisions are linked to prudence and accruals.

2 Bookkeeping: documents, books and double entry

Business documents: what each one is and why it is sent
  • A purchase order is sent by the buyer to the seller to ask for goods; it is not entered in the books because no sale has happened yet.
  • A sales invoice is sent by the seller to a credit customer to show the goods supplied, the price, any trade discount and the amount owed; the buyer files it as a purchase invoice.
  • A credit note is sent by the seller to the customer when goods are returned or the customer was overcharged; it reduces what the customer owes.
  • A statement of account is sent by the seller, usually at the end of the month, summarising the opening balance, invoices, credit notes (returns) and payments, and the balance still owed.
  • A remittance advice is sent by the customer with (or to announce) a payment, showing which invoices are being paid.
  • A bank statement is sent by the bank and shows every receipt into and payment out of the business bank account, including items such as interest, charges and credit transfers.
  • A cheque counterfoil (stub) is kept by the business as its record of a cheque it has written; a receipt is evidence that cash was paid.

Open the lesson and practice

Which ledger holds which account
  • The ledger is split into three parts: the receivables ledger, the payables ledger and the nominal (general) ledger.
  • The receivables (sales) ledger holds the personal accounts of credit customers (trade receivables, once called debtors).
  • The payables (purchases) ledger holds the personal accounts of credit suppliers (trade payables, once called creditors).
  • The nominal (general) ledger holds all other accounts: sales, purchases, returns, expenses, income, non-current assets, equity, loans and drawings.
  • Day books and the general journal are books of prime entry, not ledgers.
  • A credit transaction with a customer or supplier touches both a personal account and a nominal account (for example, goods returned to a credit supplier: debit the supplier in the payables ledger, credit purchases returns in the nominal ledger).
  • Only credit sales of goods go to the receivables ledger; a cash sale or the sale of a non-current asset does not create a customer account there.

Open the lesson and practice

Why businesses give trade discount and cash discount
  • Trade discount is a reduction from the list price given to trade customers (other businesses) and to customers who buy in bulk.
  • Trade discount is taken off at the time of sale on the invoice and is never recorded in the ledger; only the net amount is entered.
  • Cash discount (settlement discount) is a reduction offered to credit customers if they pay within an agreed time, for example 5% if paid within 30 days.
  • Cash discount is only taken if payment is made inside the time limit; it has nothing to do with paying in notes and coins.
  • Trade discount helps the seller win larger orders, keep customers coming back and grow sales.
  • Cash discount helps the seller get cash in sooner, improving liquidity, and lowers the risk that the debt becomes irrecoverable.

Open the lesson and practice

Discount columns in the cash book: where the totals go
  • A three-column cash book has columns for discount, cash and bank on each side.
  • Discount allowed is given to credit customers when they pay early, so it sits on the debit (receipts) side next to the money received.
  • Discount received is given to us by credit suppliers when we pay early, so it sits on the credit (payments) side next to the money paid.
  • The discount columns are memorandum columns only: they are not part of the double entry inside the cash book and are never balanced.
  • At the end of the period the debit-side discount total is debited to the discount allowed account (an expense).
  • At the end of the period the credit-side discount total is credited to the discount received account (income).
  • The individual discounts are also entered in each customer's or supplier's personal account, on the opposite side to the nominal posting.

Open the lesson and practice

Why the petty cash in the box differs from the book
  • The balance in the petty cash book should equal the cash counted in the petty cash box.
  • A difference can come from a recording error, such as a voucher entered at the wrong amount or not entered at all.
  • A voucher may be missing, so a payment was made but not recorded.
  • Cash may have been lost or stolen.
  • A timing difference can occur, for example the top-up from the main cashier has been recorded but not yet received, or cash was paid out before the voucher was written.

Open the lesson and practice

Completing an invoice or credit note
  • Each line total on an invoice is quantity multiplied by unit price.
  • The line totals are added to give the subtotal (the gross amount at list price).
  • Trade discount is a percentage of the subtotal and is deducted on the invoice; the result is the net total the customer owes.
  • Cash discount is only shown as a term (for example 5% for payment within 30 days); it is not deducted on the invoice.
  • A credit note is laid out like an invoice and uses the same trade discount rate as the original sale, so the customer is credited the net amount.
  • An invoice also shows the seller's name and address, the customer's name and address, the date and an invoice number.

Open the lesson and practice

Debit and credit for one transaction
  • Every transaction is entered twice: once on the debit side of one account and once on the credit side of another, for the same amount.
  • Debit the account that receives value: an asset that increases, an expense, or drawings.
  • Credit the account that gives value: an asset that decreases, income, a liability that increases, or equity.
  • Buying goods for resale is recorded in the purchases account (debit), not in inventory.
  • Drawings (cash, bank or goods taken by the owner) are debited to the drawings account.
  • Goods taken by the owner for personal use: debit drawings, credit purchases (the goods were bought, not sold).
  • When the owner pays a business cost with private money, debit the expense or asset and credit equity, because the owner has put more into the business.

Open the lesson and practice

Defining capital and revenue expenditure
  • Capital expenditure: money spent buying or improving non-current assets that the business will use for more than one year.
  • Revenue expenditure: money spent on the everyday running of the business, whose benefit is used up within the current year.
  • Capital expenditure is shown as a non-current asset in the statement of financial position (and is spread over the asset's life by depreciation).
  • Revenue expenditure is charged as an expense in the income statement of the year it is incurred.
  • Examples of capital expenditure: a motor vehicle, machinery, premises, an extension, delivery and installation of a new machine.
  • Examples of revenue expenditure: wages, fuel, repairs, maintenance, insurance, rent.
  • A good 'difference' answer compares both sides in one sentence using 'whereas'.

Open the lesson and practice

Is it capital or revenue expenditure?
  • Capital expenditure is spending to buy a non-current asset, get it ready for use, or improve it; it is recorded in the statement of financial position.
  • Revenue expenditure is spending on running the business and keeping assets working; it is an expense in the income statement.
  • Costs to get an asset ready are capital: purchase price, delivery, installation, legal fees on buying premises, and first-time fittings such as shelving or signwriting on a new van.
  • Running costs are revenue: fuel, insurance, repairs, maintenance, repainting, staff wages, interest on a loan, and inventory bought for resale.
  • Improvements and extensions (for example a factory extension) are capital; repairs that restore an asset to how it was are revenue.
  • Costs that cover future periods of running, like an extended warranty or a maintenance contract, are revenue.
  • When totals are asked, add the capital items and the revenue items separately; multiply any 'per year' cost by the number of years covered if the question requires it.

Open the lesson and practice

Matching each transaction to its source document (and book)
  • A source document is the original piece of evidence for a transaction; entries in the books are made from it.
  • Credit sale: sales invoice (copy) into the sales day book. Credit purchase of goods: purchase invoice into the purchases day book.
  • Goods returned by a customer: credit note sent to the customer, into the sales returns day book. Goods returned to a supplier: credit note received from the supplier (or a debit note we send), into the purchases returns day book.
  • Money received by credit transfer, bank interest received and bank charges are shown on the bank statement; a credit transfer from a customer may also come with a remittance advice. All go in the cash book.
  • A cheque the business writes is evidenced by its cheque counterfoil; cash received or paid is evidenced by a receipt (or till roll for cash sales); both go in the cash book.
  • Buying a non-current asset on credit is evidenced by a purchase invoice but is first entered in the journal, not the purchases day book.
  • Small cash spending is evidenced by a petty cash voucher and entered in the petty cash book.

Open the lesson and practice

Normal balances: debit side or credit side
  • Debit balances: assets, expenses, drawings and returns inwards (sales returns).
  • Credit balances: liabilities, income, equity, returns outwards (purchases returns) and provisions (depreciation and irrecoverable debts).
  • Discount allowed is an expense (debit); discount received is income (credit).
  • Carriage inwards and carriage outwards are both expenses, so both are debit balances.
  • Other receivables (prepayments) are assets (debit); other payables (accruals) are liabilities (credit).
  • The bank account can be either side: a debit balance means money in the bank; a credit balance means an overdraft.
  • A debit balance on the cash account means that amount of cash is in hand; cash can never be overdrawn.
  • A trial balance lists each balance in the column of its normal side: debit balances in the debit column, credit balances in the credit column.

Open the lesson and practice

The accounting equation and the dual effect
  • The accounting equation is: Assets = Liabilities + Equity (equity is also called capital).
  • It can be rearranged: Equity = Assets - Liabilities, and Liabilities = Assets - Equity.
  • 'Equity - Liabilities = Assets' is NOT a correct form; the equation never subtracts liabilities from equity.
  • Assets are resources the business controls (premises, inventory, trade receivables, cash); liabilities are amounts it owes (trade payables, loans, overdraft).
  • Every transaction has a dual effect: two items change and the equation still balances.
  • Buying a non-current asset on credit increases assets and increases liabilities by the same amount.
  • Returning goods to a credit supplier reduces trade payables (and reduces inventory or purchases).

Open the lesson and practice

What goes in the owner's equity account
  • The equity (capital) account records the owner's investment in the business.
  • Credit side: opening capital, any extra capital the owner pays in, and the profit for the year.
  • Debit side: drawings (cash or goods taken by the owner) and a loss for the year.
  • Profit (or loss) for the year is the final figure from the income statement and is transferred to equity at the year end.
  • Gross profit is only a line part-way down the income statement; it is not transferred to equity.
  • Profit or loss on disposal of a non-current asset is an income statement item; it reaches equity only as part of the profit for the year.

Open the lesson and practice

Why keep a petty cash book
  • A petty cash book records small cash payments made from a float kept in the office, separate from the main cash book.
  • It keeps the main cash book free of many small entries, so it is easier to use and check.
  • It lets the chief cashier hand small payments to a junior member of staff, saving the cashier's time and training the junior.
  • Its analysis columns group spending by type (for example postage, travel, cleaning), so totals can be posted to the ledger in one go.
  • Under the imprest system the float is topped up to the same amount, which helps control and check small spending.
  • A drawback is extra work and the risk of loss or theft of cash; a very small business may not need one.

Open the lesson and practice

Choosing the book of original entry
  • A book of original entry (book of prime entry) is where a transaction is first recorded before it is posted to the ledger.
  • Sales day book: goods sold on credit. Purchases day book: goods for resale bought on credit.
  • Sales returns day book: goods returned by credit customers. Purchases returns day book: goods returned to credit suppliers.
  • Cash book: all money received or paid through the bank or in cash, including credit transfers, standing orders and dishonoured cheques; discount allowed and discount received are also first recorded here when payment is made.
  • Petty cash book: small cash payments, such as postage, cleaning and travel, made from the petty cash float.
  • Journal: anything not covered by the other books, such as non-current assets bought or sold on credit, writing off irrecoverable debts, correcting errors, opening entries and year-end transfers.

Open the lesson and practice

The imprest system: topping up petty cash
  • Under the imprest system the petty cashier starts each period with a fixed amount called the float (or imprest).
  • At the end of the period the float is restored by giving the petty cashier exactly the amount spent.
  • Amount to restore = total spent in the period = float minus cash left.
  • The top-up is debited to the petty cash book (account) and credited to the bank (or cash) column of the main cash book.
  • So in the main cash book the top-up appears on the credit (payments) side.
  • After restoring, the petty cash balance is back to the full float.

Open the lesson and practice

Writing up day books and returns books
  • The sales day book lists credit sales; the purchases day book lists credit purchases of goods for resale.
  • The sales returns and purchases returns day books list goods returned on credit.
  • Each entry shows the date, the name of the customer or supplier and the net amount after trade discount.
  • Cash sales and cash purchases are not entered in day books; they go in the cash book.
  • Cash discount is never shown in a day book.
  • Returns are valued at list price less the same trade discount given on the original sale or purchase.
  • Each book is totalled at the end of the period; the total is posted to the ledger.

Open the lesson and practice

Nominal accounts posted from the books of prime entry
  • Nominal accounts (sales, purchases, expenses) are kept in the nominal ledger and receive totals from the books of prime entry, not individual invoices.
  • The sales day book total is credited to the sales account; the purchases day book total is debited to the purchases account.
  • Cash sales are credited to the sales account from the cash book; cash purchases are debited to purchases from the cash book.
  • Sales returns and purchases returns have their own accounts; they are not entered in the sales or purchases account.
  • Each analysis column total in the petty cash book (for example postage or travel) is debited to that expense account.
  • At the year end, sales and purchases are closed by a transfer to the income statement, which is shown as the balancing entry so both sides total the same.
  • If the question says 'balance the account', show 'Balance c/d' and 'Balance b/d' instead of a transfer.

Open the lesson and practice

Preparing an analysed petty cash book
  • The petty cash book has a receipts column on the left, then date and details, then a total payments column and analysis columns for each type of expense.
  • The opening balance (balance b/d) and any money received from the main cashier go in the receipts column.
  • Each payment is entered twice on the payments side: once in the total column and once in its analysis column.
  • The analysis columns must add across to the total column.
  • Under the imprest system, the amount restored equals the total spent, received from the bank (or cash) on the last day.
  • The balance c/d is the float; both sides total the same, and the balance b/d is shown on the first day of the next month.

Open the lesson and practice

Working out amounts after trade and cash discount
  • Net invoice amount = list price minus trade discount; this is the amount recorded in the books of both seller and buyer.
  • Cash discount is calculated on the net invoice amount (after trade discount), not on the list price.
  • Cash discount is only deducted if payment is made within the period stated in the terms.
  • Amount paid within the period = net amount minus cash discount; after the period, the full net amount is paid.
  • A discount rate = discount divided by the amount it was taken from, times 100.
  • Cash discount is recorded later, in the discount columns of the cash book, when payment is made.

Open the lesson and practice

Opening journal entries and other one-off journal entries
  • The general journal records transactions that do not belong in any other book of prime entry, such as opening balances and buying non-current assets on credit.
  • Each journal line shows the date, the account name and the amount in either the debit or the credit column; a narrative (a short reason starting 'Being...') follows if asked.
  • In an opening journal entry, every asset is debited and every liability is credited.
  • Equity (capital) is the balancing figure: equity = total assets minus total liabilities, and it is credited.
  • A bank overdraft is a liability, so it is credited; cash at bank and petty cash are assets, so they are debited.
  • When the owner pays for business assets with private money, the assets are debited and the amount is added to equity (credit).
  • Buying a non-current asset on credit: debit the asset account and credit the supplier's account (not the purchases account).
  • Total debits must equal total credits for every journal entry.

Open the lesson and practice

Why the capital/revenue split matters
  • Revenue expenditure is charged against profit in the year it happens; capital expenditure is recorded as a non-current asset and charged gradually through depreciation.
  • If capital expenditure is wrongly treated as revenue, expenses are too high, so profit for the year is understated and non-current assets are understated.
  • If revenue expenditure is wrongly treated as capital, expenses are too low, so profit for the year is overstated and non-current assets are overstated.
  • Correct treatment applies the matching (accruals) concept: costs are matched with the revenue of the periods that benefit from them.
  • Wrong treatment means the financial statements do not give a true and fair view, which can mislead the owner, lenders and the tax authorities.
  • A full answer gives reasons, develops each one, and ends with a conclusion.

Open the lesson and practice

Preparing a three-column cash book
  • A three-column cash book has discount, cash and bank columns on each side: debit (receipts) on the left and credit (payments) on the right.
  • A positive opening balance goes on the debit side; a bank overdraft is brought down on the credit side.
  • Discount allowed goes in the debit discount column next to the receipt; discount received goes in the credit discount column next to the payment.
  • The discount columns are not balanced, just totalled, because they are not part of the double entry in the cash book.
  • A contra entry (cash paid into the bank, or cash withdrawn from the bank) appears on both sides: for cash banked, debit bank and credit cash.
  • A dishonoured cheque is a payment out of the bank on the credit side, in the name of the customer.
  • Standing orders and direct debits are payments on the credit side of the bank column; credit transfers received are on the debit side.
  • Cash and bank columns are balanced separately and the balances brought down on the next day.

Open the lesson and practice

Writing up customer and supplier accounts
  • A ledger account has two sides, each with Date, Details and $ columns; debit on the left, credit on the right.
  • Customer account (receivables ledger): debit the opening balance owed, credit sales and dishonoured cheques; credit cash or bank received, discount allowed, sales returns and irrecoverable debts written off.
  • Supplier account (payables ledger): credit the opening balance owed and credit purchases; debit payments made, discount received and purchases returns.
  • In the details column write the name of the other account or the book of prime entry, for example 'Sales day book', 'Purchases returns day book', 'Bank' or 'Discount received'.
  • Trade discount is taken off before anything is recorded; only the net amount appears in the account.
  • Cash (settlement) discount is recorded as a separate line next to the payment; the payment plus the discount clears the debt.
  • To balance: total both sides, put the difference on the smaller side as 'Balance c/d', write equal totals, then bring the balance down on the other side on the next day as 'Balance b/d'.
  • A customer's account can have a credit balance (or a supplier's a debit balance) when the person overpaid, paid in advance, or returned goods after paying.

3 Adjustments: depreciation, irrecoverable debts, accruals

Why non-current assets lose value (causes of depreciation)
  • Depreciation is the fall in value of a non-current asset (such as machinery, motor vehicles or equipment) over its useful life.
  • Wear and tear: the more an asset is used, the more it wears out (for example a delivery van's engine and tyres).
  • Passage of time: some assets lose value simply because time passes, even if they are not used much (for example a 10-year lease runs out).
  • Obsolescence: an asset becomes out of date because newer, better technology appears or it no longer suits the business (for example an old computer).
  • Depletion: a natural resource is used up as it is extracted (for example a mine or a quarry).
  • Each cause you state must be a different idea; 'wear and tear' and 'usage' count as one idea, not two.

Open the lesson and practice

Double entry for irrecoverable debts and the provision
  • To write off an irrecoverable debt: debit the irrecoverable debts account, credit the customer's account (or the trade receivables ledger control account).
  • The irrecoverable debts account is an expense; at the year end it is transferred to the income statement.
  • To create or increase a provision: debit the income statement (or irrecoverable debts expense), credit the provision for irrecoverable debts account.
  • To decrease a provision: debit the provision for irrecoverable debts account, credit the income statement.
  • A journal entry lists the date, the account debited first with its amount, then the account credited (indented) with its amount.
  • A narrative is a short sentence explaining the entry, for example 'Write-off of a debt that will not be paid'; include it only when the question asks.

Open the lesson and practice

Other receivables and other payables: what they are and where they go
  • Other payables (current liabilities): expenses owing at the year end (accruals) and income received in advance.
  • Other receivables (current assets): expenses paid in advance (prepayments) and income still owing to the business.
  • An expense account with a debit balance brought down shows a prepayment; a credit balance shows an expense owing.
  • An income account (such as rent receivable) with a credit balance brought down shows income received in advance; a debit balance shows income owing.
  • Both appear in the statement of financial position: other receivables under current assets, other payables under current liabilities.
  • Tip: ask 'who owes whom?'. If the business owes something (money or a service), it is a payable; if it is owed something, it is a receivable.

Open the lesson and practice

Why businesses charge depreciation
  • The main purpose of depreciation is to spread the cost of a non-current asset over the years of its useful life.
  • Depreciation is NOT a way of saving cash to replace the asset, and it does NOT show the asset's market value.
  • Accruals (matching) concept: the cost of using an asset is charged in the same years as the revenue the asset helps to earn.
  • Prudence concept: depreciation stops profit and the value of non-current assets in the statement of financial position from being overstated.
  • Each year's depreciation is an expense in the income statement, so it reduces profit for the year.
  • Carrying value (net book value) = cost minus accumulated depreciation; this is the figure shown in the statement of financial position.

Open the lesson and practice

Credit control and whether to sell on credit
  • Credit control means managing customers who buy on credit so that they pay in full and on time.
  • Ways to encourage prompt payment: check customers' credit history first, set credit limits, offer a cash discount for early payment, send regular statements and reminders, charge interest on late payment, and stop supplying customers who do not pay.
  • Selling only for cash removes the risk of irrecoverable debts completely.
  • Advantages of selling on credit: attracts more customers, so revenue and profit may rise.
  • Disadvantages of selling on credit: cash comes in later (worse cash flow/liquidity) and some customers may never pay (irrecoverable debts reduce profit).
  • An evaluation weighs both sides for the business described and ends with a clear decision.

Open the lesson and practice

Calculating depreciation and carrying value
  • Straight line: annual charge = (cost - residual value) / useful life in years, or cost x the % rate when a % of cost is given.
  • Reducing balance: annual charge = rate % x carrying value at the start of that year; the charge falls every year.
  • Carrying value (net book value) = cost - accumulated depreciation (all depreciation charged so far).
  • Read the time policy carefully, for example 'a full year in the year of purchase and none in the year of disposal', or monthly (time-apportioned) charges.
  • Accumulated depreciation is the total of all the yearly charges up to that date.
  • Depreciate each asset separately when the question gives more than one, then add if a total is asked for.

Open the lesson and practice

Calculating the provision for irrecoverable debts
  • First take any debts to be written off away from trade receivables; the provision is based on what is left.
  • Provision required = rate % x trade receivables after write-offs.
  • Change in provision = new provision - old (opening) provision.
  • If the new provision is bigger, it is an increase (an expense, which reduces profit); if smaller, it is a decrease (added to profit).
  • Rate of provision = provision / trade receivables x 100.
  • Only the change in the provision goes to the income statement, not the whole new balance.

Open the lesson and practice

Irrecoverable debts and the provision for irrecoverable debts: why and how they differ
  • An irrecoverable debt is a particular customer's debt that the business is sure will never be paid, so it is written off.
  • A provision for irrecoverable debts is a best guess, often worked out as a % of trade receivables, of how much customers in general may fail to pay; no particular customer is named.
  • Prudence concept: a business must not show its profit or its assets at more than they are really worth, and it should allow for likely losses.
  • Accruals (matching) concept: the expected loss from irrecoverable debts belongs in the same year as the credit sales that created the debts.
  • In the statement of financial position, trade receivables are shown less the provision, so they are not overstated.
  • Creating or increasing a provision reduces profit for the year; writing off a debt also reduces profit and trade receivables.

Open the lesson and practice

Why accruals, prepayments and owner's own use are adjusted (concepts)
  • Accruals (matching) concept: income and expenses are recorded in the period they relate to, not when cash is paid or received.
  • An expense owing (accrual) is added to the year's expense; an expense paid in advance (prepayment) is taken off it.
  • Without these adjustments, profit for the year would be wrong and current assets or current liabilities would be missing or misstated.
  • Business entity concept: the business is treated as separate from its owner.
  • Goods or cash taken by the owner for personal use are drawings, not business expenses; goods taken reduce purchases (or cost of sales).
  • Correct adjustments give a true and fair view and let users compare one year with another.

Open the lesson and practice

Provision for irrecoverable debts account
  • The provision for irrecoverable debts account normally has a credit balance.
  • The opening balance is on the credit side as 'Balance b/d'.
  • An increase in the provision is credited, with the detail 'Income statement' (it is an expense).
  • A decrease in the provision is debited, with the detail 'Income statement' (it adds to profit).
  • The new (required) provision is carried down on the debit side as 'Balance c/d' and brought down on the credit side the next day.
  • Written-off debts do NOT go through this account; they go to the irrecoverable debts account.

Open the lesson and practice

Choosing between straight line and reducing balance, and changing method
  • The two methods named in the specification are the straight line method and the reducing balance (diminishing balance) method.
  • Straight line: the same depreciation charge every year (cost less residual value, divided by useful life, or cost times a fixed %).
  • Reducing balance: a fixed % of the carrying value each year, so the charge is highest in year 1 and falls every year after.
  • Reducing balance suits assets that lose most value early or need more repairs as they age (such as motor vehicles); straight line suits assets used evenly over their life.
  • Higher depreciation means lower profit for the year and a lower carrying value of non-current assets in the statement of financial position.
  • Consistency concept: once a method is chosen it should be kept from year to year, so results can be compared; change only with a good reason.

Open the lesson and practice

Expense and income accounts with accruals and prepayments
  • Expense account: opening prepayment is a debit Balance b/d; opening amount owing is a credit Balance b/d.
  • Expense account: cash paid is debited (Cash book/Bank); closing amount owing is a debit Balance c/d; closing prepayment is a credit Balance c/d.
  • Income account (e.g. rent receivable): opening income in advance is a credit Balance b/d; opening income owing is a debit Balance b/d; cash received is credited.
  • The balancing figure is the transfer to the income statement: the true expense or income for the year.
  • Expense for the year = cash paid - opening owing + opening prepaid + closing owing - closing prepaid.
  • After totalling, bring the closing balances down on the first day of the next year on the opposite side to where they were carried down.
  • Goods taken by the owner (for example stationery) are credited to the account with the detail 'Drawings'; unused consumables at the year end are carried down like a prepayment.

Open the lesson and practice

Provision for depreciation account
  • The provision for depreciation account holds accumulated depreciation for a class of assets; its normal balance is a credit.
  • The opening balance is on the credit side as 'Balance b/d' on the first day of the year.
  • The year's depreciation charge is credited, with the detail 'Income statement'; the expense side is debited in the income statement.
  • When an asset is sold, the depreciation on that asset is debited to this account with the detail 'Disposal' (it moves to the disposal account).
  • Close the account with 'Balance c/d' on the debit side on the last day of the year, total both sides, then show 'Balance b/d' on the credit side on the first day of the next year.
  • Every entry needs a date, a detail (the name of the other account) and an amount.

Open the lesson and practice

Disposal of non-current assets: cost, disposal and profit or loss on sale
  • When an asset is sold, its cost and its accumulated depreciation are moved into a disposal account so the gain or loss can be found.
  • Disposal account debit: the asset's cost (credit the cost account, detail 'Disposal').
  • Disposal account credit: the depreciation on that asset up to the sale (debit the provision for depreciation account) and the sale proceeds (debit cash book/bank).
  • If the debit side is bigger than the credits, the balancing figure is a loss on disposal (credit side, detail 'Income statement'); if the credits are bigger, it is a profit (debit side, detail 'Income statement').
  • Quick check: profit or loss = proceeds - carrying value, where carrying value = cost - depreciation to date.
  • In the cost account: opening balance b/d (debit), purchases (debit, cash book/bank), the cost of the asset sold (credit, Disposal), then balance c/d and b/d.
  • Apply the time policy (for example, full year in year of purchase, none in year of disposal) to find depreciation to date on the asset sold.

Open the lesson and practice

4 Control processes

Bank terms and ways of paying
  • Standing order: the payer instructs the bank to pay a fixed amount to someone at regular intervals.
  • Direct debit: the payer authorises the receiver (payee) to collect payments from the account; the amounts and dates can vary.
  • Credit transfer (bank giro credit / electronic transfer): money paid straight into the business's bank account by someone else.
  • Dishonoured cheque: a cheque the bank refuses to pay, e.g. not enough money in the drawer's account, unsigned, out of date, or words and figures differ.
  • Unpresented cheque: a cheque written and entered in the cash book that has not yet been paid out by the bank.
  • Outstanding (uncredited) lodgement: money paid into the bank and entered in the cash book that the bank has not yet recorded.
  • From the bank's point of view: a credit balance on the bank statement means the bank owes the business (money in the bank); a debit balance means an overdraft, so the bank is a creditor of the business.

Open the lesson and practice

Reading the balance on a control account
  • The normal closing balance on a trade receivables ledger control account is a debit: the total amount owed to the business by credit customers.
  • The normal closing balance on a trade payables ledger control account is a credit: the total amount the business owes to credit suppliers.
  • A control account can have balances on both sides at once, because a few individual accounts may be on the 'wrong' side.
  • A debit balance on the payables control account means a supplier owes the business: for example the business overpaid an invoice, paid in advance, or returned goods after paying.
  • A credit balance on the receivables control account means the business owes a customer: for example the customer overpaid, paid in advance, or returned goods after paying.
  • These opposite-side balances are brought down separately; they are not netted off.

Open the lesson and practice

Types of error and whether the trial balance shows them
  • Error of omission: a transaction is left out of the books completely (no debit and no credit).
  • Error of commission: the right amount is posted to the wrong account of the SAME kind, e.g. one customer's account instead of another's, or rent instead of stationery.
  • Error of principle: the right amount is posted to the wrong KIND of account, e.g. a non-current asset bought recorded as an expense, or expenses charged to drawings.
  • Error of original entry: the wrong amount is recorded in the book of prime entry, so both sides carry the same wrong figure.
  • Complete reversal: the correct accounts and amount are used, but the debit and credit are swapped.
  • Compensating errors: two separate mistakes of the same amount cancel each other out.
  • These six errors do NOT stop the trial balance agreeing; transposition of figures on both sides is also hidden.
  • Errors that DO stop agreement include one-sided postings, a wrong amount on one side only, a wrong side for one entry, and adding up a book of prime entry or an account incorrectly.

Open the lesson and practice

Which items go in a control account, and on which side
  • A control account mirrors the individual personal accounts, so an item goes on the same side as it would in a customer's or supplier's account.
  • Trade receivables control - debit side: opening debit balance, credit sales, dishonoured (returned) cheques from customers, interest charged to customers, refunds paid to customers, closing credit balance c/d.
  • Trade receivables control - credit side: opening credit balance, receipts from credit customers, discount allowed, returns inwards (sales returns), irrecoverable debts written off, contra (set-off) with payables, closing debit balance c/d.
  • Trade payables control - credit side: opening credit balance, credit purchases, interest charged by suppliers, refunds received from suppliers, closing debit balance c/d.
  • Trade payables control - debit side: opening debit balance, payments to suppliers, discount received, returns outwards (purchases returns), contra (set-off) with receivables, closing credit balance c/d.
  • Cash sales, cash purchases, provision for irrecoverable debts and discount from the other ledger are NOT entered in a control account.
  • A contra arises when the same firm is both a customer and a supplier; the smaller balance is set off, debited in payables control and credited in receivables control.

Open the lesson and practice

Why prepare a bank reconciliation statement?
  • A bank reconciliation statement explains why the bank balance in the business's cash book differs from the balance on the bank statement on the same date.
  • It is prepared by the business (the trader), not by the bank, and it is not part of the double entry.
  • It brings the cash book up to date by revealing items only the bank knew about, such as standing orders, direct debits, bank charges, interest and credit transfers.
  • It reveals errors, either in the cash book or made by the bank, so they can be corrected.
  • It shows timing differences: unpresented cheques and outstanding (uncredited) lodgements, and very old cheques that may need cancelling.
  • It helps deter and detect fraud, because the bank statement is an independent record to check against.
  • Advice questions need a clear recommendation, e.g. to continue preparing one regularly.

Open the lesson and practice

Preparing a trial balance
  • A trial balance lists every balance on one date in two columns: debit and credit; the two totals should be equal.
  • Debit balances are assets, expenses, drawings, purchases, returns inwards, carriage (inwards or outwards), discount allowed and opening inventory.
  • Credit balances are equity (capital), liabilities (bank loan, mortgage, bank overdraft, trade payables), revenue, other income (rent received, discount received), returns outwards and provisions (depreciation, irrecoverable debts).
  • The trade receivables ledger control balance is a debit (an asset); the trade payables ledger control balance is a credit (a liability).
  • A bank overdraft is a credit balance even though a bank balance in hand is a debit.
  • Opening inventory (inventory at the start of the year) goes in the debit column; closing inventory is not in the trial balance unless the question says so.
  • If the question tells you to enter a difference, put the missing amount in a suspense account on the side that is short, and label it 'Suspense'.
  • When redrafting an incorrect trial balance, keep the amounts and move any balance that is on the wrong side.

Open the lesson and practice

Why keep a trial balance? Benefits and limits
  • A trial balance is a list of every ledger account balance on one date, with debit balances in one column and credit balances in the other.
  • Its main job is to test the arithmetic of the double entry: if every debit has a matching credit, the two column totals will be equal.
  • It gathers all the balances in one place, so the income statement and the statement of financial position can be drawn up quickly.
  • If the totals do not agree, the business knows at least one mistake exists and can open a suspense account while it looks for it.
  • Agreeing totals do NOT prove the books are correct: errors of omission, commission, principle, original entry, complete reversal and compensating errors leave the totals equal.
  • A trial balance is a list, not an account, and it is not part of the double entry itself.
  • In an evaluate, discuss or advise answer you must give a benefit, a limitation where asked, and a final judgement (for example, keep preparing it).

Open the lesson and practice

Why keep control accounts?
  • A control account is a summary account that holds the totals of all the individual customer (or supplier) accounts in one place.
  • The trade receivables ledger control account summarises the receivables (sales) ledger; the trade payables ledger control account summarises the payables (purchases) ledger.
  • Control accounts are written up from totals in the books of prime entry (sales day book, cash book, returns day books, journal), not from the personal ledger itself.
  • Its closing balance should equal the total of the list of individual balances; if it does, the ledger is arithmetically correct.
  • It gives a ready total of trade receivables or trade payables for the trial balance and statement of financial position.
  • It helps prevent and detect fraud when it is kept by a different member of staff from the clerk who keeps the personal ledger.
  • It helps locate errors: if the trial balance fails to agree, comparing control totals shows which ledger holds the mistake.
  • Limitation: like the trial balance, it does not reveal every error, for example a posting to the wrong customer's account.

Open the lesson and practice

Preparing a bank reconciliation statement
  • A bank reconciliation statement starts from the UPDATED cash book balance and adjusts for timing differences to arrive at the bank statement balance (or the reverse).
  • Starting from the cash book: ADD unpresented cheques (the bank has not yet taken the money out) and DEDUCT outstanding lodgements (the bank has not yet added the money).
  • Starting from the bank statement, the signs are reversed: deduct unpresented cheques and add outstanding lodgements.
  • An overdraft is shown as a negative figure, in brackets, and the same add/deduct rules apply.
  • Only timing differences appear in the statement; bank-only items such as standing orders and charges are first entered in the cash book.
  • Every line needs a label (e.g. 'Unpresented cheque', 'Outstanding lodgement', 'Balance per bank statement') as well as the amount.
  • The final figure must equal the balance shown on the bank statement; this proves the reconciliation.

Open the lesson and practice

Updating the cash book from the bank statement
  • Before reconciling, the cash book (bank column) is updated with items that are on the bank statement but not yet in the cash book.
  • Debit side (money in): credit transfers and electronic receipts from customers, bank interest received, and corrections of receipts that were understated.
  • Credit side (money out): standing orders, direct debits, bank charges, overdraft interest charged, dishonoured cheques from customers, and corrections of payments that were understated.
  • A dishonoured cheque is credited in the cash book because the money the business recorded as received did not arrive.
  • Unpresented cheques and outstanding lodgements are already in the cash book; they are NOT entered again - they go in the reconciliation statement.
  • An opening credit balance in the cash book is an overdraft; an opening debit balance is money in the bank.
  • After posting, balance the account (Balance c/d) and bring the updated balance down on the next day (Balance b/d).

Open the lesson and practice

Preparing a trade receivables or trade payables control account
  • A control account uses the normal T-account layout: Date, Details, $ on each side, balanced at the end of the period.
  • Trade receivables control: debit opening debit balance, credit sales (sales day book), dishonoured cheques, refunds to customers, interest charged; credit opening credit balance, receipts (cash book), discount allowed, returns inwards (sales returns day book), irrecoverable debts, contra.
  • Trade payables control: credit opening credit balance, credit purchases (purchases day book), refunds from suppliers, interest charged by suppliers; debit opening debit balance, payments (cash book), discount received, returns outwards (purchases returns day book), contra.
  • Ignore items that do not belong: cash sales, cash purchases, the other ledger's discounts and returns, and provision for irrecoverable debts.
  • If the question says some customers or suppliers are on the unusual side at the end, carry that amount down as a separate balance on the opposite side.
  • Balance the account: the bigger side total goes on both sides, the balancing figure is 'Balance c/d', and it is brought down as 'Balance b/d' on the first day of the next period.
  • Details name the source or the other account, e.g. 'Sales day book', 'Cash book', 'Returns inwards day book', 'Discount allowed'.

Open the lesson and practice

The suspense account
  • A suspense account is opened when the trial balance totals do not agree, to hold the difference until the errors are found.
  • It lets the trial balance be totalled (and draft statements prepared) and it helps in correcting the errors.
  • The opening entry goes on the side that is SHORT in the trial balance: if the debit total is smaller, the suspense balance is a debit.
  • Only corrections of errors that affected the trial balance pass through suspense; the other errors are corrected between two ordinary accounts.
  • Each suspense correction is the opposite leg of a journal: if the journal credits Suspense, the suspense account shows the entry on its credit side, with the other account's name as the detail.
  • When all the errors are corrected, the suspense account totals agree and it has no balance left.

Open the lesson and practice

Journal entries to correct errors
  • A correcting journal has the account to debit first, then the account to credit (indented), with the amount in the debit and credit columns; a narrative is only written if asked.
  • To correct an error, compare what WAS done with what SHOULD have been done, then post only the difference.
  • Errors that left the double entry balanced (omission, commission, principle, original entry, complete reversal, compensating) are corrected without the suspense account.
  • Errors that left the trial balance out (one-sided postings, wrong amount on one side, wrong side, undercasts or overcasts) need the suspense account as one leg of the correction.
  • A complete reversal is corrected with TWICE the amount: once to cancel the wrong entry and once to make the right one.
  • An amount posted to the wrong side of an account is also corrected with twice the amount in that account, with suspense taking the other leg.
  • An original entry error is corrected for the difference between the correct and the recorded amounts, in both accounts affected.
  • Errors in individual customer or supplier names are corrected in those personal accounts (e.g. debit Parker, credit Palmer).

5 Financial statements, ratios and manufacturing

The three inventories of a manufacturer
  • A manufacturer holds three kinds of inventory: raw materials, work in progress and finished goods.
  • Raw materials are bought to be turned into products and have not yet been used in production.
  • Work in progress is goods that are partly made at the end of the year.
  • Finished goods are completed products ready to be sold.
  • Raw materials and work in progress inventories are used in the manufacturing account; finished goods inventories are used in the income statement.
  • A manufacturer may buy in finished goods when it cannot make enough itself, for example because of production problems or high demand.

Open the lesson and practice

Where does this item go in the financial statements?
  • Income and expenses for the year go to the income statement; assets, liabilities and equity go to the statement of financial position.
  • Non-current assets are kept for use in the business for more than a year (premises, machinery, vehicles); accumulated depreciation is deducted from them in that section.
  • Current assets are inventory (including work in progress), trade and other receivables, bank and cash, including the petty cash balance.
  • Current liabilities are due within 12 months: trade payables, other payables and a bank overdraft; a bank loan due after more than a year is a non-current liability.
  • Irrecoverable debts written off are an expense in the income statement; the provision for irrecoverable debts is deducted from trade receivables under current assets.
  • Capital expenditure appears in the statement of financial position; revenue expenditure appears in the income statement.
  • A trade receivables control account balance is a current asset; a trade payables control account balance is a current liability.

Open the lesson and practice

Ratio formulas and how to calculate them
  • Gross profit percentage = gross profit / revenue x 100.
  • Profit for the year as a percentage of revenue = profit for the year / revenue x 100.
  • Return on capital employed (ROCE) = profit for the year / capital employed x 100.
  • Current (working capital) ratio: divide current assets by current liabilities, and write it as x : 1.
  • Liquid (acid test) ratio: take inventory away from current assets, then divide by current liabilities, and write it as x : 1.
  • The three profitability ratios are percentages; the two liquidity ratios are written as a ratio to 1.
  • Trade receivables in current assets are shown after taking off the provision for irrecoverable debts.

Open the lesson and practice

Evaluating profitability using ratios
  • Profitability means how well a business turns its sales or the capital invested into profit.
  • Gross profit percentage changes with selling prices and the cost of buying or making goods.
  • Profit for the year as a % of revenue also reflects how well expenses are controlled.
  • If gross profit percentage is steady but profit for the year as a % of revenue falls, expenses have grown faster than sales.
  • Return on capital employed shows how efficiently the capital invested earns profit; it falls if extra capital is put in without a matching rise in profit.
  • A good evaluation states the movement, gives a likely reason and finishes with an overall judgement.

Open the lesson and practice

Liquidity and working capital
  • Liquidity is a business's ability to pay its short-term debts when they fall due; profitability is its ability to make profit from its activities.
  • Working capital = current assets - current liabilities.
  • A current ratio of about 2 : 1 and a liquid ratio of about 1 : 1 are usual benchmarks.
  • A ratio well below the benchmark means the business may struggle to pay suppliers; a ratio far above it may mean too much is tied up in inventory, receivables or cash.
  • A big gap between the current and liquid ratios means a lot of inventory is held.
  • Credit suppliers worry about poor liquidity because they may be paid late or not at all.
  • Ways to improve liquidity include collecting debts faster, getting longer credit from suppliers, holding less inventory, and the owner putting in more capital.

Open the lesson and practice

Will profit (or working capital) go up, down or stay the same?
  • Profit only changes when an income or an expense account changes.
  • More income or less expense raises profit; more expense or less income lowers it.
  • Capital introduced, cash drawings, buying a non-current asset and paying a debt in full do not change profit (apart from any depreciation that follows).
  • If an expense is left out (an accrual not recorded, depreciation omitted), profit is overstated; if a prepayment is ignored, the expense is too high and profit is understated.
  • Treating capital expenditure as revenue expenditure understates profit; treating revenue expenditure as capital overstates it.
  • Gross profit only changes with revenue, purchases, returns, carriage inwards, goods taken by the owner and inventory; other expenses affect profit for the year only.
  • Working capital is current assets less current liabilities; it changes only when net current assets change: a swap between two current assets (or two current liabilities), or an equal rise in a current asset and a current liability, leaves it unchanged.

Open the lesson and practice

Calculating a revised profit
  • Start from the draft profit and correct it one item at a time.
  • An expense that was missed or understated is subtracted; an expense that was overstated is added back.
  • Income received in advance belongs to next year, so it is subtracted from this year's profit.
  • Goods taken by the owner reduce purchases (cost of sales), so recording them adds back to profit.
  • Creating or increasing a provision for irrecoverable debts is an expense; reducing it adds to profit.
  • Only the part of a bill that belongs to this year is adjusted (time apportion an invoice that crosses the year end).
  • Capital spending wrongly charged as an expense is added back, but the extra depreciation on it is then subtracted.

Open the lesson and practice

Preparing a manufacturer's income statement
  • A manufacturer's income statement uses production cost (from the manufacturing account) in place of purchases.
  • Cost of sales = opening inventory of finished goods + production cost + any finished goods bought in - closing inventory of finished goods.
  • Only finished goods inventories go in the income statement; raw materials and work in progress stay in the manufacturing account.
  • Shared costs (rent, insurance, electricity, depreciation) are apportioned: the factory share goes to the manufacturing account and the office share to the income statement.
  • Office expenses are adjusted for accruals and prepayments before they are apportioned.
  • Administration wages, carriage outwards and selling costs are income statement expenses; production and supervisor wages are not.
  • Gross profit = revenue - cost of sales; profit for the year = gross profit - administrative and selling expenses.

Open the lesson and practice

Preparing a statement of financial position after adjustments
  • A statement of financial position shows assets, equity and liabilities at one date (at 31 March ...), and total assets must equal total equity and liabilities.
  • Non-current assets are shown at cost less accumulated depreciation (including this year's charge) to give the carrying amount.
  • Current assets are inventory, trade receivables less the provision for irrecoverable debts, other receivables (prepayments), and cash and bank balances in hand.
  • Current liabilities are amounts due within a year: trade payables, other payables (accruals) and a bank overdraft.
  • Non-current liabilities are amounts due after more than a year, such as a long-term bank loan.
  • Closing equity = opening equity + profit for the year - drawings (+ any capital introduced).
  • A credit balance on the bank account is an overdraft, a current liability, not a negative asset.

Open the lesson and practice

Preparing an income statement for a trader or service business
  • An income statement covers a period (for the year ended ...) and shows gross profit and then profit for the year.
  • Revenue is sales less returns inwards; cost of sales is opening inventory plus purchases (less returns outwards, plus carriage inwards) less closing inventory.
  • Gross profit is revenue less cost of sales; profit for the year is gross profit plus other income less expenses.
  • Carriage inwards belongs in cost of sales; carriage outwards is a selling expense below gross profit.
  • An amount owing (accrual) is added to the expense paid; an amount paid in advance (prepayment) is taken off it, so only this year's cost is charged.
  • Depreciation for the year is an expense: straight line spreads cost less residual value evenly over the useful life; reducing balance applies a percentage to the carrying value (cost less accumulated depreciation).
  • Goods taken by the owner are drawings, so their cost is taken out of purchases; buying a non-current asset is capital expenditure and never an expense.
  • Irrecoverable debts and any increase in the provision for irrecoverable debts are expenses; a decrease in the provision is extra income.

Preparing and correcting a manufacturing account
  • The manufacturing account works out the cost of making the goods finished in the year (production cost).
  • Cost of raw materials consumed = opening raw materials + purchases (+ carriage inwards) - closing raw materials.
  • Prime cost = raw materials consumed + direct (production) wages + direct expenses such as royalties.
  • Factory overheads are indirect factory costs: supervisor wages, factory rent, power, insurance, and depreciation of factory machinery, each adjusted for accruals and prepayments and apportioned where shared.
  • Production cost = prime cost + factory overheads + opening work in progress - closing work in progress.
  • Office costs, selling costs and carriage outwards do not belong in the manufacturing account; they go to the income statement.
  • Production cost is carried to the income statement in place of purchases.

6 Partnerships and incomplete records

Partnership agreements and what happens without one
  • A partnership agreement is a written document that sets out the rules the partners have agreed.
  • It usually covers the profit-sharing ratio, interest on capital, interest on drawings, partners' salaries and interest on partners' loans.
  • If there is no agreement, section 24 of the Partnership Act 1890 applies.
  • Under the Act, profits and losses are shared equally; there are no salaries, no interest on capital and no interest on drawings.
  • Under the Act, a partner who lends money to the business (beyond capital) receives interest of 5% per year on the loan.
  • An agreement is advisable because partners can refer to it if they disagree, which avoids disputes.

Why keep a full set of accounting records
  • A full set of records means every transaction is recorded by double entry in books of prime entry and ledgers.
  • Full records allow financial statements to be prepared quickly and as often as needed, because the figures are ready.
  • Accurate, up-to-date figures help the owner make better decisions.
  • They help control trade receivables (chasing customers) and trade payables (paying on time).
  • A trial balance can be drawn up, which helps find some errors; banks and tax authorities also trust complete records.
  • The drawback is the extra time and cost of keeping them.

Open the lesson and practice

Why partners get interest, salaries and loan interest
  • Interest on capital rewards partners for the money they have invested, which is fair when capitals are unequal.
  • Interest on drawings is charged to partners to discourage them from taking too much out of the business.
  • A partnership salary rewards a partner for extra work or responsibility.
  • Interest on a partner's loan is an expense of the business, charged before profit for the year, and credited to the partner's current account.
  • A partner may prefer to lend rather than add capital because a loan is repaid and earns a fixed return.
  • Partnerships need an appropriation account to share profit, and separate capital and current accounts for each partner, because more than one owner is involved.

Finding equity and profit when records are incomplete
  • Equity (capital) = total assets - total liabilities. This is the accounting equation rearranged.
  • A statement of affairs is a list of assets and liabilities at a date, used to find equity when full records are not kept.
  • Non-current assets are included at carrying amount (cost less accumulated depreciation).
  • A bank overdraft is a liability, not an asset.
  • Profit = closing equity - opening equity + drawings - extra capital introduced.
  • If closing equity is lower than expected after these adjustments, the result is a loss.

Should a trader form a partnership (or an LLP)?
  • A partnership is two or more people who run a business together and share its profits and losses.
  • Main benefits of taking on a partner: more capital is brought in, the new partner adds skills and ideas, and the workload and risks are shared.
  • Main drawbacks: profits must be shared, the original owner loses full control of decisions, and partners may disagree.
  • In an ordinary partnership each partner has unlimited liability, so their personal possessions can be taken to pay business debts.
  • A limited liability partnership (LLP) is a separate legal entity, so each partner can lose only the amount invested in it.
  • Evaluate, explain and advise questions always need a clear final decision that follows from the points made.

Finding missing sales, purchases and balances
  • Credit sales can be found from a trade receivables control account: closing balance + receipts from customers (+ discounts allowed, returns, irrecoverable debts) - opening balance.
  • Credit purchases can be found from a trade payables control account: closing balance + payments to suppliers (+ discounts received, returns) - opening balance.
  • Total sales = credit sales + cash sales; total purchases = credit purchases + cash purchases.
  • A missing opening or closing balance is the balancing figure of the same control account.
  • Receipts from customers come from the bank (and cash) summary, not the income statement.
  • Mark-up or margin can be used to find sales from cost of sales when no receipts are given.

Open the lesson and practice

Partners' current and capital accounts
  • Each partner has a capital account (money invested, usually unchanged) and a current account (day-to-day amounts owed to or by the partner).
  • The credit side of a current account records what the partner earns: opening credit balance, interest on capital, salary, share of profit and interest on a partner's loan.
  • The debit side records what the partner takes or owes: drawings, interest on drawings, a share of loss and any opening debit balance.
  • Drawings are debited to the partner's current account.
  • A credit balance means the business owes the partner money; a debit balance means the partner has taken out more than they earned.
  • Balance the account with 'Balance c/d' on the last day and bring it down as 'Balance b/d' on the next day.

Partnership appropriation account
  • The appropriation account shows how the profit for the year is shared between the partners.
  • Interest on drawings is added to profit, because partners pay it to the business.
  • Interest on capital and partners' salaries are deducted from profit; they are shares of profit, not expenses.
  • The amount left (the residual profit) is shared in the profit-sharing ratio, and the shares must add back to that amount.
  • Interest on a partner's loan is not an appropriation: it is an expense in the income statement (debit), with the partner's current account credited.
  • If the draft profit is wrong (e.g. missing depreciation or loan interest), correct it first to get the profit for the year.

Partnership statement of financial position
  • Assets and liabilities are set out exactly as for a sole trader: non-current assets, current assets, total assets, then equity and liabilities.
  • Non-current assets are shown at carrying amount (cost less accumulated depreciation); land at valuation is shown at that value.
  • Trade receivables are shown after deducting the allowance for doubtful debts.
  • The equity section shows each partner's capital account and current account balances in separate columns, with a total.
  • A debit balance on a current account is deducted in the equity section.
  • A partner's loan is a liability: current if repayable within 12 months, non-current otherwise. A bank overdraft is a current liability.

Income statement from incomplete records
  • Revenue and purchases must first be derived from receipts, payments and opening and closing receivables and payables.
  • Cost of sales = opening inventory + purchases - closing inventory.
  • Gross profit = revenue - cost of sales; profit for the year = gross profit + other income - expenses.
  • Expenses are adjusted for accruals and prepayments: add amounts owing at the end, deduct amounts prepaid at the end (and reverse opening ones).
  • Depreciation must be calculated (straight-line on cost, reducing balance on carrying amount) and shown as an expense.
  • Drawings are never an expense; they do not appear in the income statement.

Statement of financial position from incomplete records
  • Opening equity is found from the opening assets less opening liabilities.
  • Closing equity = opening equity + additional capital + profit for the year - drawings (cash and goods taken).
  • If profit is not given, it can be found as the balancing figure: closing net assets - opening equity - new capital + drawings.
  • Non-current assets are shown at carrying amount after this year's depreciation.
  • Loans repayable within 12 months are current liabilities; loans repayable later are non-current liabilities.
  • A bank overdraft is a current liability; cash in hand is a current asset.
