Idea. A furniture factory buys wood, pays its workers and runs its machines. It needs to know what it cost to make the goods finished this year. That figure is the production cost, worked out in the manufacturing account. Direct costs go straight into each product: raw materials, production wages and royalties. Together they make prime cost. Indirect factory costs, such as the supervisor's wages and factory rent, are factory overheads. Office costs, selling costs and carriage outwards are not factory costs. They go to the income statement, and so do finished goods inventories.

Method. Write the heading. Then take opening raw materials, add purchases, and subtract closing raw materials. That is the cost of raw materials consumed. Add direct wages and royalties to reach prime cost. List each factory overhead. Adjust it for any amount owing or prepaid first, then take the factory share. Include depreciation of factory machinery. Total them and add to prime cost. Last, add opening work in progress and subtract closing work in progress. The result is production cost. In a correction question, start from the draft figure and add or subtract each error.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2023, Paper 2. Prepare Dingle's manufacturing account for the year ended thirty-first January 2023. Eleven marks. Here is the first table Dingle gave. It shows machinery, office equipment and inventories at the start and the end of the year. And here is the second table: the other balances at 31 January 2023, including the wages in three parts. Raw materials were twenty-one thousand two hundred at the start and twenty-three thousand six hundred at the end. Purchases were one hundred and ninety-eight thousand and forty-five. So raw materials consumed are one hundred and ninety-five thousand six hundred and forty-five. Add production wages of eighty-nine thousand seven hundred and royalties of forty-one thousand three hundred. Prime cost is three hundred and twenty-six thousand six hundred and forty-five. Now the shared overheads. Take eighty per cent of rent and insurance, and ninety per cent of general expenses. Now depreciation. The question says factory machinery is depreciated at fifteen per cent a year, straight line, and a full year is charged in the year of purchase. So the new machines count in full: use the cost at the year end, one hundred and twenty-eight thousand. Fifteen per cent of that is nineteen thousand two hundred. Factory power has three thousand three hundred owing, so add it. The supervisor's wages have eight hundred and thirty paid in advance, so take it off. Add the machinery depreciation. The overheads total one hundred and fifty-nine thousand three hundred and ten. Prime cost plus overheads is four hundred and eighty-five thousand nine hundred and fifty-five. Work in progress fell by seven hundred, so add seven hundred. Production cost is four hundred and eighty-six thousand six hundred and fifty-five. Common mistake. A common mistake is putting work in progress inside prime cost, or the wrong way round. Add the opening figure and subtract the closing figure, after the overheads. Another is including office costs, such as office equipment depreciation or administration wages. Only factory costs go here.

Your turn. Now you try. Riva makes candles. Use the data to calculate her prime cost and her production cost. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. Raw materials consumed are twenty-nine thousand. Add wages: prime cost is forty-seven thousand. Overheads are eight thousand rent plus four thousand power, twelve thousand. Work in progress fell by five hundred, so production cost is fifty-nine thousand five hundred. Remember: raw materials and direct costs make prime cost, then add factory overheads, then adjust for work in progress. Now try the practice questions.
