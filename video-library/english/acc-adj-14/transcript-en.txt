Idea. A shop's rent is one thousand dollars a month, so the rent for the year is twelve thousand. By the year end it has paid only eleven thousand. It still owes one thousand. The expense for the year is still twelve thousand, not what was paid. In an expense account, the opening prepayment, the cash paid and the closing amount owing go on the debit side. The opening amount owing and the closing prepayment go on the credit side. The gap is the income statement figure.

Method. Here is the method. Enter the opening balances on the first day. Enter the cash paid. Enter the closing balances carried down on the last day. The balancing figure is the transfer to the income statement. Then total both sides, and bring the closing balances down the next day, on the opposite side.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2024. On 1 May 2023, Alex owed wages of one thousand five hundred and eighty, and had paid nine hundred and twenty in advance. During the year he paid eighty-seven thousand nine hundred. On 30 April 2024, he owed four thousand two hundred and thirty. Five marks. Wages are an expense. Wages paid in advance go on the debit side, nine hundred and twenty. Wages owing go on the credit side, one thousand five hundred and eighty. Both are balance brought down, on 1 May 2023. The wages paid during the year, eighty-seven thousand nine hundred, go on the debit side, from the bank. At the year end Alex still owes four thousand two hundred and thirty. It belongs to this year, so carry it down on the debit side. That adds it to this year's expense. The debit side totals ninety-three thousand and fifty. The credit side must match. So the income statement figure is ninety-three thousand and fifty, minus one thousand five hundred and eighty: ninety-one thousand four hundred and seventy. Bring the four thousand two hundred and thirty down on the credit side on 1 May 2024: wages still owing. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme. Check with a formula. Cash paid, minus the opening amount owing, plus the opening prepayment, plus the closing amount owing. That gives ninety-one thousand four hundred and seventy again.

Common mistake. A common mistake is using the cash paid as the expense. The expense is the balancing figure. Another is putting an opening prepayment on the credit side, or bringing a balance down on the same side it was carried down.
