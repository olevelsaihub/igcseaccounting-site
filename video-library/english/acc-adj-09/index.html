Double entry for irrecoverable debts and the provision

Idea. A customer owes a business four hundred and fifty dollars, then closes down. The money will never come. The business must stop showing it as money owed. This is called writing off the debt. Every entry has a debit and a credit. To write off a debt, debit the irrecoverable debts account, which is an expense. Credit the customer's account, because they no longer count as owing us. A provision is different. To create or increase it, debit the income statement and credit the provision account. To decrease it, do the opposite.

Method. A journal entry shows the date, then the account debited first, with its amount in the debit column. The account credited goes on the next line, set a little to the right, with its amount in the credit column. So: decide if it is a write-off or a change in the provision. Write the debit account first, then the credit account. Put the amount in both columns. Add a narrative only if the question asks.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2024. Shahid's trade receivables include a debt of four hundred and fifty dollars that is irrecoverable. We must prepare a journal entry to write it off. No narrative is needed. Two marks. First decide. One customer will never pay, so this is a known loss: a write-off. The provision account is not used. Debit first: irrecoverable debts, four hundred and fifty dollars. It is an expense, and expenses are debits. Then credit the trade receivables ledger control account, four hundred and fifty. Customers now owe less. That is one mark for each line. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is reversing the entry. Trade receivables go down, so they are credited, not debited. Another is crediting the provision when writing off a debt. The provision is only for the estimate. A known loss goes through the irrecoverable debts account.
