Idea. Imagine a cash tin in the office with two hundred and fifty dollars in it. Staff take small amounts for stamps, taxis and cleaning, and leave a slip each time. At the end of the month, you sort the slips into groups to see where the money went. That is an analysed petty cash book. Each payment goes in the total column, and again in its analysis column. So the analysis columns always add across to the total.

Method. The book has a receipts side on the left, like a debit side, and a payments side on the right. Start with the opening balance in the receipts column, with the date and the words balance brought down. Enter each payment with its date and details, once in the total column and once in its analysis column. Then add every column and check the analysis totals add across to the total. Last, the top-up. Under the imprest system, the business gives back exactly the amount spent, as a receipt from the bank. Then the balance carried down is the full float, both sides total the same, and the balance comes down next month.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2025. Tariq paid two hundred and fifty dollars into petty cash on 1 January. We must prepare the petty cash book for January, balance it and bring the balance down. Nine marks. Here are the six payments for January. On the thirty-first, Tariq tops up petty cash from the bank to restore the opening balance. The opening two hundred and fifty goes in receipts. Then the six payments go on the payments side, each in the total column. Together they come to one hundred and ten dollars. Now the analysis. Postage stamps, stationery and printer ink go to postage and stationery: forty-eight. The taxi and train fares go to travel: thirty-two. The cleaner goes to cleaning: thirty. Forty-eight, thirty-two and thirty add up to one hundred and ten. On the thirty-first, one hundred and ten comes in from the bank, in receipts. The balance carried down is two hundred and fifty. Both sides total three hundred and sixty, and two hundred and fifty comes down on 1 February. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is putting the top-up from the bank on the payments side. Money coming into petty cash is a receipt. Another is carrying down the cash left before the top-up, one hundred and forty, instead of the restored float of two hundred and fifty.
