Irrecoverable debts and the provision for irrecoverable debts: why and how they differ

Idea. Imagine you run a shop and let customers pay at the end of the month. Most of them pay. But one customer moves away and never pays. That money is lost. Accountants use two different terms. An irrecoverable debt is one particular customer's debt that we are sure will never be paid, so it is written off. A provision for irrecoverable debts is a best guess of how much customers in general may fail to pay. No customer is named. Writing off a debt, or creating a provision, reduces profit for the year. And in the statement of financial position, trade receivables are shown less the provision, so they are not shown too high.

Method. Two concepts explain why. Prudence: never show profit or assets at more than they are really worth, and allow for likely losses. Accruals, or matching: the expected loss belongs in the same year as the credit sales that created the debts. Here is the method. Decide: a known loss, or an estimate? Name the concept and say what it means. Apply it: what happens to profit and trade receivables? If the question says discuss or evaluate, finish with a clear conclusion.

Worked example. Here is a real question from November 2024. Romala sells goods on credit only. Should she create a provision for irrecoverable debts? We must discuss, using accounting concepts. Five marks. Point one: prudence. Romala sells only on credit, so some customers may never pay. A provision recognises that likely loss now. Without it, her profit for the year and her trade receivables would be shown too high. Point two: accruals, or matching. The possible losses come from this year's credit sales. So the expected loss should be charged in the same year as the revenue from those sales. Finally, a conclusion. Yes, Romala should create the provision, because her financial statements will give a more realistic view. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is saying a provision is money saved in the bank. It is not. It is an estimate, recorded in the books, that lowers profit and receivables. Another is naming prudence and stopping there. Always say what would be wrong without the provision: profit and receivables would be overstated.

Your turn. Now you try. Lena runs a sports shop. One customer has gone bankrupt, and she expects some other debts may not be paid. Which item is which, and which concept applies? Pause here and work it out.

Answer. Tom's three hundred dollars is an irrecoverable debt: a named customer and a known loss, so Lena writes it off. The two per cent is a provision: nobody is named, it is only an estimate. Prudence: Lena should allow for likely losses. Otherwise her profit for the year and her trade receivables would be overstated. So she should write off Tom's debt and create the provision. Remember: known loss or estimate, then the concept and its effect, then a conclusion. Now try the practice questions.
