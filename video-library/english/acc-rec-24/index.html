Why the capital/revenue split matters

Idea. A café buys a new coffee machine that will last for years. It also pays for a repair to the old one. The machine helps for years to come. The repair is used up now. Buying or improving a non-current asset is capital expenditure. It goes to the statement of financial position and is charged gradually through depreciation. Running costs, such as repairs, are revenue expenditure. They are charged against this year's profit.

Method. Why does the split matter? Get it wrong, and two figures go wrong together. Treat capital as revenue, and profit and non-current assets are both understated. Treat revenue as capital, and both are overstated. Develop it with an example. A new computer recorded in office expenses makes expenses too high, so profit is too low. And the computer is missing from the assets, so they are too low as well. Conclude: correct treatment matches costs to the right years, so the statements give a true and fair view. Wrong figures could mislead the owner, lenders and the tax authorities.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2025. Advise Jay why it is important to differentiate between capital expenditure and revenue expenditure. Five marks. First effect: machinery repairs, such as Jay's one thousand five hundred dollars, are a revenue cost. If Jay records them as capital, his expenses are too low, so his profit for the year is overstated. Second effect: if Jay records a new machine in an expense account, profit is too low, and machinery in the statement of financial position is too low as well. Conclusion: correct classification matches each cost to the years that benefit, so Jay's statements give a true and fair view. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is getting the direction wrong. If a capital item is treated as an expense, profit is understated, not overstated. Another is only repeating the definitions. Say what goes wrong in the statements, and for whom.

Your turn. Now you try. Advise Lina. Which figures are wrong, and which way? You do not need amounts. Pause and decide.

Answer. The service is revenue expenditure, so it is correct. But the oven is capital expenditure. As an expense, it makes expenses too high, so profit is understated. And equipment is missing from non-current assets, so they are understated too. Each is too low by twenty-five thousand dollars, less this year's depreciation on the oven, which we are not told. So, Lina, move the oven to the equipment account and depreciate it. Then your statements will give a true and fair view.
