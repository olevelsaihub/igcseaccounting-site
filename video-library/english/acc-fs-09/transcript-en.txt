Idea. Picture a shop full of stock, but with almost no cash, and this week's bills are due. It may even be profitable, yet it cannot pay. Liquidity is a business's ability to pay its short-term debts when they fall due. Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities. Two ratios measure liquidity. A current ratio of about two to one, and a liquid ratio of about one to one, are the usual benchmarks.

Method. Quote both ratios and how they changed. Compare each with its benchmark, and say what it means for paying short-term debts. Then answer any extra part, such as the effect on suppliers, and give a judgement. A ratio far below the benchmark means the business may struggle to pay suppliers. A ratio far above it is not always good: money may be sitting idle in inventory or cash. A big gap between the two ratios means a lot of inventory.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2024. Evaluate the change in Louise's liquidity over two years, and the implications for her credit suppliers. Five marks. Here is the table Louise gave. The current ratio was two point eight to one in 2022 and one point two to one in 2023. The quick ratio, also called the acid test, was one point six to one, then nought point eight to one. The current ratio fell from two point eight to one, to one point two to one. It is still above one, so current assets are only just enough to meet current liabilities, but it is far below the usual two to one. The quick ratio fell from one point six to one, to nought point eight to one. That is below one to one. Without selling inventory, Louise cannot cover what she owes in the short term. For credit suppliers, Louise may pay late, or struggle to pay at all. So suppliers may refuse more credit, or insist on payment before they deliver more goods. Overall, Louise's liquidity has worsened, because both ratios have fallen. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is describing the numbers without saying what they mean. Compare with the benchmark and link it to paying debts. Another is saying a very high ratio is always good. It may mean money is tied up doing nothing.
