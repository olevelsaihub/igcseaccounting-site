Idea. A shop with two hundred credit customers cannot add up two hundred accounts every month. Instead, it posts the month's totals from its books into one account: the trade receivables ledger control account. It looks like any ledger account: debit on the left, credit on the right. What raises the amount customers owe is a debit. What lowers it is a credit.

Method. First, enter both opening balances on the first day, debit and credit separately. Then cross out items that do not belong, such as cash sales and the other ledger's discounts. If some customers have overpaid at the end, carry that amount down on the debit side. Then find the main balance, write equal totals, and bring both balances down.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2024. Prepare Jonas's trade receivables ledger control account for March, balance it, and bring the balances down. Ten marks. Here is Jonas's data from the paper. Notice two traps: cash sales, and discounts received. Neither belongs in a receivables control account. Enter the opening balances separately: forty-two thousand five hundred on the debit side, and eight hundred and fifty on the credit side. Sales, refunds to customers and dishonoured cheques raise what customers owe, so they are debits: fifty-one thousand two hundred and sixty, two hundred and fifty, and four hundred and thirty-one. Receipts, discount allowed and sales returns lower the debt, so they are credits: thirty-six thousand five hundred, six hundred and fifty, and two thousand three hundred and forty-five. One customer overpaid by one thousand two hundred. That is a credit balance, so carry it down on the debit side. The debit side totals ninety-five thousand six hundred and forty-one. The credits so far are forty thousand three hundred and forty-five. The difference, fifty-five thousand two hundred and ninety-six, is the main balance carried down. On the first of April, bring the main balance, fifty-five thousand two hundred and ninety-six, down on the debit side. Customers owe Jonas this amount. The one thousand two hundred carried down on the debit side comes down on the credit side. Jonas owes that customer. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is including cash sales or discounts received. They do not affect what credit customers owe. Another is netting the opening balances into one figure. Each balance stays on its own side.
