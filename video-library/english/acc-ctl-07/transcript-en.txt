Idea. When you check a shopping receipt, the total can be right even if one item is wrong. You paid for apples, but the till typed pears at the same price. In bookkeeping, the trial balance lists every balance. If total debits equal total credits, it agrees. But some errors keep both sides equal, so the trial balance cannot show them. Omission, commission, principle, original entry, complete reversal and compensating errors are all hidden. Errors such as posting only one side, or adding up an account wrongly, make the totals differ.

Method. Ask questions in order. First: was the transaction recorded at all? If there is no debit and no credit, it is an error of omission. Second: was the wrong amount written in the day book? Then both sides carry the same wrong figure. That is an error of original entry. Third: are the right accounts used, but the debit and credit swapped? That is a complete reversal. Fourth: was the wrong account used? If it is the same kind of account, it is commission. If it is a different kind, such as an asset recorded as an expense, it is principle. Last: two separate mistakes of the same amount that cancel each other out are compensating errors.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2023. Name the type of error in each of five scenarios. Five marks. A purchase invoice was not entered at all: omission. A cash purchase was debited to cash and credited to purchases. The right accounts, but swapped: complete reversal. Goods from Tessa were posted to Nessa's account. Both are suppliers, the same kind: commission. A motor vehicle was entered in motor expenses. An asset treated as an expense: principle. A sales invoice total was entered wrongly in the day book, so the wrong amount went to both sides: original entry. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is calling a vehicle put in motor expenses an error of commission. An asset became an expense, a different class, so it is principle. Another is thinking the trial balance reveals an omission. Nothing was recorded on either side, so the totals still agree.

Your turn. Now you try. Name the error in each case, and say whether the trial balance still agrees. Pause and decide.

Answer. Repairs are an expense, but went to an asset account: principle. Ali and Ahmed are both customers: commission. Right accounts, swapped sides: complete reversal. None of these upsets the totals, so the trial balance still agrees. Remember: recorded at all, wrong figure from the start, sides swapped, then same or different kind of account.
