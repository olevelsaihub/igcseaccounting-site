Writing up customer and supplier accounts

Idea. When a business buys or sells on credit, the money is paid later. So it keeps an account for every credit customer and every credit supplier, to know who owes what. Every ledger account has two sides. Debit on the left, credit on the right. Each side has a date, the details, and the amount. A customer owes us, so their balance and sales go on the debit side. Money received, discount allowed and returns reduce it, so they go on the credit side. A supplier's account is the mirror image.

Method. First, decide: customer or supplier? Then enter the opening balance as balance brought down: debit for a customer, credit for a supplier. Next, work out the amounts. Trade discount comes off the list price before anything is recorded, and off returns too. Cash discount is only given for paying in time. Post each transaction in date order. In the details, write the other account or book, such as bank, or purchases day book. Then balance it. Put the difference on the smaller side as balance carried down, write equal totals, and bring the balance down on the opposite side, next day.

Worked example. Here is a real question from November 2025. Sean owes his supplier Sui one thousand five hundred and forty dollars. Sui gives fifteen per cent trade discount on purchases over five hundred dollars, and five per cent cash discount for paying within ten days. We must prepare Sui's account, balance it, and bring the balance down. Eight marks. Sean owes Sui, so the opening balance goes on the credit side. On the fourth, six hundred and twenty is over five hundred, so trade discount applies: six hundred and twenty times zero point eight five is five hundred and twenty-seven. On the eighth, Sean pays the opening balance within ten days, so he takes five per cent cash discount, seventy-seven dollars, and pays one thousand four hundred and sixty-three. Both go on the debit side. On the tenth, he returns goods listed at eighty dollars. They had trade discount, so the return is sixty-eight dollars. On the twenty-fifth, he pays the rest: five hundred and twenty-seven minus sixty-eight, four hundred and fifty-nine. That is too late for cash discount. On the twenty-ninth, he buys goods for three hundred and forty. That is not over five hundred, so no trade discount. Now balance. The credit side totals two thousand four hundred and seven. The debit side has two thousand and sixty-seven. The difference, three hundred and forty, is the balance carried down, on the debit side. Bring it down on the credit side on the first of August. Sean still owes Sui three hundred and forty dollars. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is recording the list price. Take the trade discount off first, for returns too. Another is bringing the balance down on the same side. It always crosses to the other side.

Your turn. Now you try. Maya is a credit customer of Kofi. Read the transactions, then prepare Maya's account in Kofi's books. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. First the amounts. The sale is four hundred dollars after trade discount, and the return is forty. Maya paid in time, so discount allowed is forty-five, and the bank payment is eight hundred and fifty-five. Maya is a customer, so her balance and the sale are debits, one thousand three hundred in total. Bank, discount and returns are credits. The balance, three hundred and sixty dollars, is brought down on the debit side on 1 June. Remember: customer or supplier, net amounts, post each line, then balance and bring it down.
