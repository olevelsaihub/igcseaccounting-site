Idea. Two friends run a phone-case stall together. Both put money in. At the end of the year, each wants to know: how much of the business is mine? A partnership's statement of financial position looks like a sole trader's. Only equity is different. Each partner has a capital account and a current account, shown in columns, with a total. The capital account holds the money each partner put in. The current account changes every year. It grows with interest on capital, salary and profit share. It falls with drawings and interest on drawings.

Method. Step one: work out each current account balance. Then build the statement. Non-current assets at carrying amount. Current assets, with trade receivables less the allowance. Then equity in columns, and liabilities. A loan from a partner is a liability, not equity. It is current if it is repaid within twelve months, and non-current if later. A bank overdraft is always a current liability.

Worked example. Here is a real question from June 2025. Prepare Victoria and Albert's statement of financial position at thirtieth April 2025. Fifteen marks. Here is the trial balance they gave, in two parts. The first part has the allowance, bank, capital accounts, closing inventory, current accounts, drawings and fixtures and fittings. And the second part: land and buildings, motor vehicles, other receivables and payables, Albert's loan, petty cash, the profit for the year, and trade receivables and payables. Both sides total two hundred and twenty thousand, two hundred and twenty dollars. First, read the agreement. Profits are shared equally, with five per cent interest on capital and on drawings, and a salary for Victoria. There is no interest on partnership loans. Without that line, the Partnership Act of eighteen ninety would give Albert five per cent on his loan. First the profit shares. Add interest on drawings of one hundred and fifty. Take off interest on capital, four thousand, and Victoria's salary, twelve thousand. That leaves sixty-four thousand one hundred and fifty: thirty-two thousand and seventy-five each. Now each current account. Albert started with a debit of two thousand three hundred, so it is a minus. Victoria's closing balance is forty-eight thousand one hundred and eighty-five. Albert's is thirty thousand and fifteen. Assets next. Land at valuation, fixtures and vehicles at carrying amount: one hundred and fifty-five thousand. Current assets, with receivables less the allowance, are thirty-four thousand four hundred and twenty. Total assets: one hundred and eighty-nine thousand four hundred and twenty. Equity in columns. Capital accounts total eighty thousand. Current accounts total seventy-eight thousand two hundred. Total equity is one hundred and fifty-eight thousand two hundred. Albert's loan is repaid within a year, so it is a current liability, with payables and the overdraft: thirty-one thousand two hundred and twenty. Equity plus liabilities is one hundred and eighty-nine thousand four hundred and twenty, the same as total assets. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is adding a debit current account balance. It is the partner owing the business, so take it away. Another is putting a partner's loan in equity. A loan is a liability. Check its repayment date.

Your turn. Now you try. Prepare the equity section for Sara and Tom, and decide where Tom's loan goes. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. Capital accounts total sixty-five thousand. Tom's debit current account is taken away, so current accounts total two thousand five hundred. Sara's total is forty-three thousand six hundred, Tom's twenty-three thousand nine hundred: sixty-seven thousand five hundred in all. Tom's loan is repaid in 2030, more than twelve months away. So it is a non-current liability, not part of equity. Remember: current accounts first, assets at carrying amount, equity in partner columns, and loans with the liabilities.
