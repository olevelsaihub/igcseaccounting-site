Preparing a three-column cash book

Idea. A business keeps money in two places: the till and the bank. A cash book records both, with a cash column and a bank column. Money in goes on the debit side, money out on the credit side. A three-column cash book adds a discount column on each side. Discount allowed sits on the debit side, discount received on the credit side. These columns are only totalled, never balanced. An overdraft is a credit balance. Cash paid into the bank is a contra, entered on both sides. A dishonoured cheque, standing orders and direct debits take money out of the bank.

Method. First, the opening balances. Cash goes on the debit side. Bank goes on the debit side if there is money in it, the credit side if overdrawn. Next, enter receipts on the debit side and payments on the credit side, naming the other account in the details. With cash discount, enter the money actually paid, and the discount on the same line. Finally, total the discount columns, and balance cash and bank separately: carry down on the last day, bring down on the first day of next month.

Worked example. Here is a real question from November 2025. Kate keeps a three-column cash book. Enter her August transactions, balance it, and bring the balances down. Ten marks. Kate starts with cash of one hundred and twelve dollars, on the debit side, and a bank overdraft of four hundred and twenty-five, on the credit side. On August the second, Kate pays R O H four hundred and ninety-four after five per cent discount. That is ninety-five per cent of the invoice, so the invoice was five hundred and twenty: discount received, twenty-six. On the debit side: cash sales of eight hundred and thirty-five, and the contra, seven hundred and ninety-five into the bank. Alzate pays nine hundred and eighty less five per cent: discount forty-nine, bank nine hundred and thirty-one. On the credit side: the overdraft, R O H, and Slater's dishonoured cheque, three hundred and twenty, taken back out of the bank. Then the contra in cash, the standing order of two hundred and twenty, and the cleaner, thirty-five. Balance each column separately. Cash: nine hundred and forty-seven in, eight hundred and thirty out, leaving one hundred and seventeen. Bank: one thousand seven hundred and twenty-six in, one thousand four hundred and fifty-nine out, leaving two hundred and sixty-seven. Both balances are carried down on the credit side, the smaller side, so each column totals the same on both sides. Both balances come down on the debit side on the first of September: cash one hundred and seventeen, bank two hundred and sixty-seven. Common mistake. A common mistake is putting an opening overdraft on the debit side. It is owed to the bank, so it is a credit. Another is entering a contra on one side only. It always appears on both sides.
