Idea. If you lend a friend ten dollars, your friend owes you. But if you pay a shop twice by mistake, the shop owes you. A balance always tells you who owes whom. A business with many credit customers adds all their accounts together in one account. This is a control account. There is one for receivables and one for payables. The normal balance on the receivables control account is a debit: the total that credit customers owe the business. On the payables control account, it is a credit: the total the business owes its credit suppliers.

Method. First, name the account: receivables means customers, payables means suppliers. Then say who owes whom. A control account can have a balance on both sides at once. Most suppliers are owed money, but one or two may owe us. Both balances are brought down separately. They are never netted off. For a balance on the unusual side, think of the debt being turned round. Someone paid too much, paid too early, or returned goods after paying. Write each reason as a short, separate point.

Worked example. Here is a real question from November 2024. On the first of April, Saima's trade payables ledger control account had six thousand seven hundred dollars credit, and three hundred and fifty dollars debit. State two reasons for a debit balance. Two marks. This is the payables account, so the normal balance is the credit. Six thousand seven hundred dollars is what Saima owes her suppliers. The debit of three hundred and fifty means some suppliers owe Saima. So how can a supplier owe Saima? The debt has been turned round. First reason: Saima overpaid a supplier, paying more than the invoice. Second reason: she paid a supplier in advance, before the goods arrived. Returning goods after paying would also earn the mark. This explanation is by olevels dot com, not an official mark scheme.

Common mistake. A common mistake is swapping customers and suppliers. A debit on the payables account means suppliers owe the business, not the other way round. Another is netting the two balances into one figure. Each balance is brought down on its own side.
