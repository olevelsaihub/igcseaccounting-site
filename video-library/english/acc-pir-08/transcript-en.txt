Idea. You run a tuck shop where friends can pay later. At the start of the week, friends owed you twenty dollars. During the week they paid you fifty. At the end, they owe you thirty. How much did you sell on credit? Fifty, minus twenty, plus thirty: sixty dollars. Many small traders keep incomplete records: a bank summary, and lists of who owes what. We rebuild the missing figures, such as sales and purchases, from them.

Method. Picture a total trade receivables account. The opening balance is a debit. Receipts, discounts allowed and returns are credits. The closing balance goes in as balance carried down. The figure that makes it balance is credit sales. Purchases work the same way, in a trade payables account. Payments, discounts received, returns and the closing balance, minus the opening balance, give credit purchases. If the question asks for total sales, add the cash sales. Total purchases add the cash purchases.

Worked example. Here is a real question from January 2023. Calculate Yamini's credit sales for the year ended thirtieth September 2022. Three marks. Trade receivables were twenty-one thousand four hundred on the first of October 2021, at the start of the year, and thirty-two thousand five hundred on the thirtieth of September 2022, at the end. The bank summary shows one hundred and sixty-seven thousand six hundred and fifty received from customers. Put them in the account. Opening receivables on the debit side. Receipts from the bank and the closing balance on the credit side. The credit side totals two hundred thousand one hundred and fifty. Take away the twenty-one thousand four hundred. Credit sales are one hundred and seventy-eight thousand seven hundred and fifty dollars. The thirty-two thousand five hundred is brought down as next year's opening debit. Common mistake. A common mistake is adding the opening balance. Those sales were made last year, so take them off. Another is forgetting discounts or returns. They also reduce what customers owe, so they go on the credit side.
