Knowledge card

Writing up customer and supplier accounts

Layout

A customer's account starts with a debit balance because the customer owes us; a supplier's account starts with a credit balance because we owe them. Post each entry with its date, the other account and the net amount, then balance it off.

  • A ledger account has two sides, each with Date, Details and $ columns; debit on the left, credit on the right.
  • Customer account (receivables ledger): debit the opening balance owed, credit sales and dishonoured cheques; credit cash or bank received, discount allowed, sales returns and irrecoverable debts written off.
  • Supplier account (payables ledger): credit the opening balance owed and credit purchases; debit payments made, discount received and purchases returns.
  • In the details column write the name of the other account or the book of prime entry, for example 'Sales day book', 'Purchases returns day book', 'Bank' or 'Discount received'.
  • Trade discount is taken off before anything is recorded; only the net amount appears in the account.
  • Cash (settlement) discount is recorded as a separate line next to the payment; the payment plus the discount clears the debt.
  • To balance: total both sides, put the difference on the smaller side as 'Balance c/d', write equal totals, then bring the balance down on the other side on the next day as 'Balance b/d'.
  • A customer's account can have a credit balance (or a supplier's a debit balance) when the person overpaid, paid in advance, or returned goods after paying.

Working

The calculation

Net amount = List price − Trade discount

Trade discount comes off first. Cash discount is then worked out on the amount owed, only if payment is made in time.

Tap a part to see what it means and how to find it.

To find Net amount:Net amount = List price × (100% − trade discount %)

To find Payment:Payment = Amount owed − Cash discount

  • Never record the list price in the account.
  • No cash discount if the payment is outside the time allowed.
  • Take trade discount off returns of discounted goods too.

Try itGoods with a list price of $900 are bought with 20% trade discount. The supplier allows 5% cash discount for payment in time. Dev pays in time. How much does Dev pay?

  1. Trade discount = 900 × 20% = 180.
  2. Net amount = 900 − 180 = $720. This is the figure in the account.
  3. Cash discount = 720 × 5% = $36.
  4. Payment = 720 − 36 = $684.
  5. Enter Bank 684 and Discount received 36 on the debit side.

The method

  1. Customer or supplier?

    A customer is in the receivables ledger; a supplier is in the payables ledger.

  2. Opening balance

    Enter Balance b/d on the first day: debit for a customer, credit for a supplier.

  3. Work out amounts

    Take trade discount off list prices and returns, and check if cash discount applies.

  4. Post in date order

    Each line needs the date, the other account or book, and the net amount.

  5. Balance off

    Total both sides, put Balance c/d on the smaller side, and write equal totals level.

  6. Bring it down

    Enter Balance b/d on the opposite side on the first day of the next month.

  7. Unusual balances

    Explain them with an overpayment, a payment in advance or goods returned after payment.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    List price recorded

    Record only the amount after trade discount, for returns too.

  • ✗
    Late cash discount

    Check the dates. No cash discount if payment is outside the time allowed.

  • ✗
    Wrong side

    Credit purchases go on the credit side of a supplier's account.

  • ✗
    Own name in details

    Write the other account or book, such as "Bank", not the person's name.

  • ✗
    Missing discount line

    Without the discount line, the payment alone will not clear the debt.

Worked explanation

  1. Sui is a supplier, so the opening balance of $1 540 owed by Sean is a credit: 'Jul 1 Balance b/d 1 540'.
  2. Jul 4: list price $620 is over $500, so 15% trade discount applies. Net purchase = 620 x 0.85 = $527. Credit 'Purchases day book 527'.
  3. Jul 8: Sean pays the 1 July balance. The goods were bought on 30 June, so payment is within 10 days and 5% cash discount applies. Discount = 1 540 x 5% = $77; bank payment = 1 540 - 77 = $1 463. Debit 'Bank 1 463' and 'Discount received 77'.
  4. Jul 10: returned goods at list price $80 from the 4 July order. That order had 15% trade discount, so the return is 80 x 0.85 = $68. Debit 'Purchases returns day book 68'.
  5. Jul 25: Sean pays the rest of the 4 July purchase: 527 - 68 = $459. This is more than 10 days after 4 July, so no cash discount. Debit 'Bank 459'.
  6. Jul 29: list price $340 is not over $500, so no trade discount. Credit 'Purchases day book 340'.
  7. Credit side total = 1 540 + 527 + 340 = $2 407. Debit entries so far = 1 463 + 77 + 68 + 459 = $2 067. Balance c/d = 2 407 - 2 067 = $340 on the debit side.
  8. Both totals are $2 407. Bring the balance down: 'Aug 1 Balance b/d 340' on the credit side (Sean still owes Sui $340).

How the marks are earned

Marks go to each correct line with its date, details and amount: the opening balance, the bank payment of $1 463, the discount of $77, the purchase of $527, the return of $68, the payment of $459, and the brought-down balance of $340. The $527, $68 and $459 lines are allowed as your own figures if an earlier discount calculation went wrong. 'Cash book' is accepted in place of 'Bank', and 'Purchases' or 'Purchases returns' are accepted in place of the day book names.
