Knowledge card

Which ledger holds which account

Choice

The ledger has three parts: the receivables ledger holds credit customers, the payables ledger holds credit suppliers, and the nominal ledger holds every other account. Day books are books of prime entry, not ledgers.

  • The ledger is split into three parts: the receivables ledger, the payables ledger and the nominal (general) ledger.
  • The receivables (sales) ledger holds the personal accounts of credit customers (trade receivables, once called debtors).
  • The payables (purchases) ledger holds the personal accounts of credit suppliers (trade payables, once called creditors).
  • The nominal (general) ledger holds all other accounts: sales, purchases, returns, expenses, income, non-current assets, equity, loans and drawings.
  • Day books and the general journal are books of prime entry, not ledgers.
  • A credit transaction with a customer or supplier touches both a personal account and a nominal account (for example, goods returned to a credit supplier: debit the supplier in the payables ledger, credit purchases returns in the nominal ledger).
  • Only credit sales of goods go to the receivables ledger; a cash sale or the sale of a non-current asset does not create a customer account there.

5 features to find

Find the features that decide which ledger each account belongs in.

Customer owes us

Why it matters
Ravi buys on credit and pays later, so he is a trade receivable.
Write it as
Ravi's account is kept in the receivables ledger.

We owe the supplier

Why it matters
Tay Supplies sells to us on credit, so it is a trade payable.
Write it as
Tay Supplies' account is kept in the payables ledger.

Sales is not a person

Why it matters
Sales, purchases and expenses are things, not people, so they are nominal accounts.
Write it as
The sales account is kept in the nominal ledger.

Assets and drawings

Why it matters
Non-current assets, equity, loans and drawings all go in the nominal ledger.
Rule
If it is not a credit customer or supplier, it is nominal.

Not a ledger

Why it matters
A day book lists transactions first. No account is kept there.

Exam tipIf an option names a day book or the journal, it cannot be where an account is kept.

The method

  1. Person or thing?

    Decide if the account is a person (customer or supplier) or a thing (sales, expense, asset, equity).

  2. Pick the personal ledger

    A credit customer goes in the receivables ledger; a credit supplier goes in the payables ledger.

  3. Everything else

    Any other account goes in the nominal ledger.

  4. Rule out books

    Cross out any day book or the journal: they are books of prime entry.

  5. Check credit and goods

    For a transaction, the personal ledgers are used only for goods bought or sold on credit.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Choosing a day book

    A day book records transactions first. Accounts are kept in a ledger.

  • ✗
    Swapping the ledgers

    Suppliers are payables, not receivables. Ask who owes whom.

  • ✗
    Sales in a personal ledger

    The sales and purchases accounts are nominal accounts.

  • ✗
    Selling an old asset

    A non-current asset sold on credit does not go in the receivables ledger.

After your attempt, see the full worked explanation

Worked explanation

  1. A credit customer buys from us and pays later, so the customer owes us money.
  2. Money owed to us is a trade receivable, so the customer's personal account is kept in the receivables ledger.
  3. The general journal is a book of prime entry, the general ledger holds nominal accounts, and the payables ledger holds suppliers. So A, B and C are wrong.
