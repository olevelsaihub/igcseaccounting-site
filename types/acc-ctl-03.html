Knowledge card

Why keep control accounts?

Written

A control account is a summary of a whole personal ledger, written up from books of prime entry by a different person, so it checks the ledger's arithmetic, deters fraud and gives a ready total.

  • A control account is a summary account that holds the totals of all the individual customer (or supplier) accounts in one place.
  • The trade receivables ledger control account summarises the receivables (sales) ledger; the trade payables ledger control account summarises the payables (purchases) ledger.
  • Control accounts are written up from totals in the books of prime entry (sales day book, cash book, returns day books, journal), not from the personal ledger itself.
  • Its closing balance should equal the total of the list of individual balances; if it does, the ledger is arithmetically correct.
  • It gives a ready total of trade receivables or trade payables for the trial balance and statement of financial position.
  • It helps prevent and detect fraud when it is kept by a different member of staff from the clerk who keeps the personal ledger.
  • It helps locate errors: if the trial balance fails to agree, comparing control totals shows which ledger holds the mistake.
  • Limitation: like the trial balance, it does not reveal every error, for example a posting to the wrong customer's account.

The method

  1. Count the marks

    A 5-mark answer usually needs two developed benefits and a conclusion.

  2. Pick benefits

    Arithmetic check, ready total, deters fraud, locates errors.

  3. Develop each one

    Add a 'because' or 'so that' link to every benefit.

  4. Explain independence

    If asked why it uses books of prime entry or separate staff, explain that the check must not come from the same source or person.

  5. Conclude

    End with a judgement or advice for the named trader.

Do not lose marks here

  • ✗
    Describing contents

    Say why it is kept, not what goes in it.

  • ✗
    "Proves it is correct"

    It does not reveal every error.

  • ✗
    Bare "prevents fraud"

    Explain that a different person keeps it.

  • ✗
    No conclusion

    Five-mark items reserve a mark for the judgement.

  • ✗
    Ledgers mixed up

    Receivables are customers; payables are suppliers.

Common mistakes: Inferred, not measured. Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

Worked explanation

  1. Question: 'Explain to Beth the benefits of maintaining a trade receivables ledger control account.' (5 marks).
  2. Plan: benefit 1 + development, benefit 2 + development, conclusion.
  3. Benefit 1: it checks the arithmetic of the receivables ledger. Development: the control balance is compared with the total of all the individual customer balances; if they match, the ledger has been added up correctly.
  4. Benefit 2: it deters fraud. Development: the control account is written up by someone other than the receivables ledger clerk, so a false entry in a customer's account would make the two totals differ.
  5. Conclusion: tell Beth the account is worth keeping.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Benefit 1: The control account acts as a check on the arithmetic of the receivables ledger. Its closing balance is compared with the total of the individual customer balances, and if the two agree Beth knows the ledger has been posted and added correctly.

Benefit 2: It discourages fraud. The control account is kept by a person other than the clerk who writes up the customers' accounts, so any dishonest change in a customer's account would cause a difference that would be noticed.

Conclusion: Keeping a trade receivables ledger control account gives Beth several useful benefits, so she should continue to maintain it.

How the marks are earned

Up to 2 marks for each benefit (1 for naming it, 1 for the linked reason) and 1 mark for the conclusion. Other benefits are accepted, such as giving a ready total of trade receivables for the statement of financial position, or helping to locate errors when the trial balance does not agree.
